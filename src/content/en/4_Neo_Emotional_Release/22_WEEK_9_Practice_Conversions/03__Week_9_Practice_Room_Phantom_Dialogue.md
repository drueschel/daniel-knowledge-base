# Week 9 Practice Room: Phantom Dialogue

## Key Concepts
- **The Conversion Transition:** After progressing through *logging in*, somatic integration, and emotion definement, the transition to the phantom dialogue is initiated with a decisive prompt: *"Is this emotion connected to a male or a female person?"*
- **Identification from Specific to General:** Inquiries begin with the most common attachment figures (*"Could it be your mother? Your father? An ex-partner?"*). If negative, questions broaden progressively to relatives, teachers, or influential figures from the past.
- **Spatial Anchoring (Setting the Frame):** Grounding the phantom in the physical room: Determining their exact spatial location, distance from the client, physical posture, and the quality of energy radiating from them.
- **The Three Dialogic Dimensions:**
  1. *Client Focus:* What did you need to say to this person in the past that could never be expressed?
  2. *Phantom Focus:* What do their eyes or body language reveal? Is there an unspoken admission or plea they carry?
  3. *Relational Dynamics Focus:* What shifts in the space between the client and the phantom once the unspoken truth is vocalized?
- **Resolving Incomplete Cycles:** Many somatic contractions and persistent psychological symptoms stem from uncompleted relational expressions; the phantom dialogue provides the safe container to voice these truths and discharge stuck survival energy.

## Summary
In Week 9, practitioners take their first practical steps into Conversions through the Phantom Dialogue. The practice session strictly follows the foundational framework established in previous modules: establishing connected awareness with the client (logging in), identifying a present-day tension or emotional block, grounding it into bodily sensations via somatic integration, and naming the emotional charge (definement). Once the feeling is clearly articulated, the conversion is initiated by asking whether the somatic charge carries a male or female association.

The practitioner then navigates the inquiry from "specific to general." Because deep-seated somatic burdens are overwhelmingly tied to primary developmental relationships, the facilitator first checks for parents or long-term romantic partners before expanding outwards to extended family, authority figures, or formative acquaintances. Once the figure is identified, the facilitator "sets the frame" by helping the client project the phantom into the physical room, clarifying where they stand, how close they are, and what their posture conveys.

The therapeutic interaction progresses across three interconnected inquiry tracks:
1. **Direct client expression:** Giving voice to buried grievances, boundaries, rage, or grief that were silenced during childhood or past relational impasses.
2. **Reading the phantom:** Witnessing the subtle emotional shifts, posture changes, or silent messages conveyed by the visualized persona.
3. **Tracking relational reciprocity:** Continually observing how each disclosure reshapes the interpersonal field—checking how the client feels after speaking and how the phantom responds.

By bringing what was once suppressed into explicit, witnessed dialogue, the nervous system completes its frozen self-protective responses. The phantom loses its overwhelming grip, allowing the client to separate from inherited burdens and reclaim relational autonomy.
