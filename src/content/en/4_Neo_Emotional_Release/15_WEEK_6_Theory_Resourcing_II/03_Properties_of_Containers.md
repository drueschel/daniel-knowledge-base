# Properties of Containers

In Neo-Emotional Release, every part of our psyche—including our inner child parts—acts as a container holding specific emotions or pain. These containers cause a temporary fragmentation, but our system inherently strives for wholeness, seeking to merge back into our larger self, or "supercontainer." Understanding how to help these fragmented parts reintegrate is foundational to the healing process.

## The Drive Towards Wholeness
Every container in the psyche is designed to deal with pain until we are ready to process it. Just as the ego longs to merge with a higher state of consciousness, every inner child part (Imprinted Consciousness Container, or ICC) yearns to reunite with the wholeness of your being. 

## Two Paths to Integration
There are two fundamental approaches in Neo-Emotional Release to help a container surrender its pain and return to a state of wholeness:

1. **Cathartic Exploration (Going In):**
   - Dive directly into the container to explore its fixations, emotions, and specific woundings.
   - By bringing your awareness and presence to these feelings—truly feeling and witnessing them—the trapped emotion is transformed and released.
   - The container's protective purpose becomes obsolete, allowing it to dissolve back into the free-flowing state of the supercontainer, much like a river flowing into the ocean.

2. **Strengthening the Supercontainer (Building Resources):**
   - Focus on building the resources and strength of the larger self (the supercontainer).
   - Cultivate a deep sense of safety, security, and love within the nervous system.
   - When the supercontainer is profoundly safe and supportive, this loving energy penetrates the fragmented container, gently helping it surrender and integrate without direct catharsis.

## The Role of the Therapist
A very young child lacks the capacity to regulate their own sensory information and emotions. When stressors accumulate—like physical discomfort or unmet needs—their internal container overflows. In these moments, a parent must step in to act as the container for the child's overwhelming emotions. 

Similarly, in therapeutic work, the practitioner acts as a larger, holding container for the client. By holding a safe space, facilitators support clients in developing their own robust internal containers, empowering them to eventually hold and process their own imprinted pain.
