## Key Concepts
- **Internal Family Systems (IFS):** A therapeutic approach and theory of the psyche developed by Richard Schwartz, proposing that the human mind naturally consists of multiple sub-personalities called "parts."
- **Protectors and Exiles:** Parts function either as *Exiles*, which carry unresolved old pain and burdens, or *Protectors*, which actively shield the system from experiencing this pain.
- **Managers vs. Firefighters:** Protectors are divided into *Managers* (proactive parts that try to control life to prevent triggers) and *Firefighters* (reactive parts that use extreme behaviors to numb emerging pain).
- **Positive Intent:** All parts, regardless of how dysfunctional or symptomatic their behavior may appear, are driven by a positive intent to protect the individual.
- **The Self:** Beyond our parts, everyone possesses a spiritual core known as the "Self." When protective parts relax and step aside, the Self emerges as an empowering, non-pathologizing presence.

## Summary

In this introductory session on Internal Family Systems (IFS), the focus is placed on understanding the fundamental theory of the human psyche as conceptualized by Richard Schwartz. At the core of IFS is the multiplicity paradigm, which suggests that our minds are not singular, monolithic entities. Instead, they are composed of various sub-personalities, or "parts," that interact with each other and take control at different times. This perspective provides a compassionate, non-pathologizing lens through which to view human behavior, allowing us to understand that even the most destructive symptoms are strategies employed by parts trying to help the system survive.

The model distinguishes between different types of parts based on their functional roles. *Exiles* are the parts trapped in the past, holding onto unresolved trauma, shame, or deep emotional pain. To keep this pain from overwhelming the system, *Protectors* step in. These protectors can be proactive *Managers*, who try to control daily life to avoid triggers (such as through perfectionism or overworking), or reactive *Firefighters*, who step in with drastic measures (like binge eating, dissociation, or self-harm) whenever an exile's pain threatens to surface. Both types of protectors share the same underlying goal: to shield the person from suffering.

A transformative element of the IFS model is the concept of the *Self*. The Self is not a part, but rather the innate, spiritual core of a person. Schwartz discovered that the Self doesn't need to be developed or found; it naturally surfaces whenever parts feel safe enough to relax and step aside. Leading from the Self allows for profound healing and a completely different way of relating to both our inner world and the people around us, setting the foundation for the deep transformational work that IFS facilitates.
