# Session 5: Final Integration of Developmental Trauma

In this final session of Module 3, Dr. Arielle Schwartz, Manuela Mischke-Reeds, and Dr. Scott Lyons integrate the teachings of the entire program. They outline the arc of a somatic trauma therapy session, discuss how to assess for early developmental wounds, and analyze a live demonstration to show the theory in practice.

## 1. The Arc of a Session

A somatic therapy session working with developmental trauma does not necessarily follow a rigid, linear structure, but generally includes these elements:

- **Arrival and Orienting:** Establishing a "safe enough" physical and relational environment. This includes negotiating proximity (e.g., "Where would you like to sit in relation to me?") and finding somatic anchors (e.g., feeling the feet on the floor, noticing a long spine).
- **Assessment:** Listening for signs of pre-perinatal or early developmental trauma.
- **Navigating the 8-Phase Map:** Moving dynamically through the phases of developmental repair based on the client's emerging somatic material.
- **Re-evaluation:** Checking in to see how the system has integrated the missing experience and looking for markers of growth.

## 2. Assessing for Early Trauma

Early developmental trauma is pre-verbal, meaning the client rarely has an explicit memory or narrative of the events. Instead, the story is held in the body.
- **Listening for Themes:** The therapist listens for overarching life themes: profound isolation, agonizing difficulty making choices, chronic power struggles, or a pervasive feeling of being "unmet" or "unseen."
- **Reading the Somatic Narrative:** The body reveals the developmental stage. A client who constantly appeases, breathes shallowly, or has a tense, locked jaw is exhibiting the somatic profile of the independence stage.
- **Trusting the Terrain:** The therapist does not need to know the specific details of the childhood events (the "twists and turns") to facilitate healing. If the therapist recognizes the *terrain* (e.g., the struggle for autonomy), they can work directly with the somatic presentation.

## 3. The Demonstration: Following the Body's Wisdom

In a live demonstration with a participant (Martha), Manuela Mischke-Reeds modeled how to track and follow somatic cues without forcing an agenda.
- **Validating the Resistance:** When the client felt a "heavy" sensation and a tight jaw, the therapist did not try to fix or release the tension immediately. Instead, she validated the tension as a necessary protection.
- **The Micro-Movements of Agency:** By exploring the tension in the jaw, the client discovered an empowering "No." The therapist supported this by saying, "I'm right here. You can take up space."
- **Holding the Ambivalence:** As the client expanded into her power (the "butterfly drying its wings"), she also felt a need for comfort and safety (the "teddy bear"). The therapist held space for both, showing that the client can have autonomy *and* connection simultaneously. 

## 4. Re-evaluation and Markers of Growth

The final phase of the map involves assessing how the client's nervous system is integrating the work. Markers of growth and successful developmental repair include:
- The ability to handle conflict or disagreement without immediately crumbling or going into an anxious threat response.
- The capacity to identify a missing need and articulate it to a loved one.
- The ability to set boundaries without paralyzing guilt.
- The ability to yield and connect with others without feeling a loss of sovereignty or selfhood.
- The capacity to hold multiple, conflicting truths at the same time (e.g., "I am angry at you, and I love you").

Healing is a spiral, not a straight line. Clients will revisit these developmental stages as deeper layers of trauma are uncovered, but with each pass, they will recover from triggers more quickly and live more fully in the present.
