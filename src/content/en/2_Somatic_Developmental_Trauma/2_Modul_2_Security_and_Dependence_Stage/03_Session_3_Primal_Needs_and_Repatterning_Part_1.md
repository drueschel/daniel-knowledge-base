# Session 3: Primal Needs and Repatterning (Part 1)

In this session, Mariah Rooney and Kai Cheng Thom shift the focus from assessing trauma to the active process of re-patterning missing experiences. They explore the distinction between micro-skills and meta-skills, the importance of somatic attunement, and how to navigate the spectrum of consent.

## 1. Meta-Skills vs. Micro-Skills

Drawing on the work of Amy Mindell, the presenters distinguish between two types of clinical skills:
- **Micro-Skills (What to do):** The specific techniques, protocols, interventions, and physical postures a practitioner uses (e.g., asking a specific question, leaning forward, using a contact statement). 
- **Meta-Skills (How to be):** The underlying orientation, attitude, and energetic stance of the practitioner (e.g., deep compassion, infinite curiosity, genuine presence). 

While micro-skills are essential, they are merely the "sacred objects" placed upon the "altar" of the meta-skill. Without the right meta-skill, even the most perfect protocol will fall flat.

## 2. Somatic Attunement and Holding Big Space

Somatic attunement is the meta-skill of being with another individual's soma in a way that is experienced as both connected and caring. It relies heavily on non-linguistic prosody (voice tone, pitch, pacing) and the practitioner's own nervous system state.

### The Myth of "Calm"
Attunement does not always mean being perfectly calm. If a client is expressing righteous rage or profound grief, a practitioner who remains completely placid may be experienced as invalidating or disconnected. True attunement means matching the client where they are at, while maintaining a grounded, centered foundation. 

### The "Scientist" and "Rescuer" Parts
Practitioners must be aware of their own parts that get triggered in session:
- **The Scientist:** Wants to dissect, analyze, and diagnose the client like a bug under a microscope to avoid the messiness of emotion.
- **The Rescuer:** Feels desperate to "save" the client or fix their pain, which often stems from the practitioner's inability to tolerate the discomfort of witnessing suffering.

To hold "big space," practitioners must ground themselves (feeling their feet, seat, and spine) and trust that the client's system has the inherent wisdom to heal. 

## 3. Contact Statements

Contact statements are a micro-skill used to bring the client's awareness to their present-moment bodily reality without imposing an interpretation. 
- **Example:** "It seems like your hands are pushing something away," or "I notice that every time we talk about this, your gaze drops to the floor."
- **Speaking in Question Marks:** Practitioners should frame their observations as curiosities rather than definitive facts, leaving room for the client to correct them (e.g., "I hear some anger in your voice, does that resonate?").

## 4. The Spectrum of Consent

Adapted from Dr. Betty Martin's "Wheel of Consent," the spectrum helps clients understand the nuance between a forced "yes" and a true "yes."
- **Enduring:** Putting up with active pain, harm, or damage.
- **Tolerating:** Putting up with an uncomfortable situation because you feel you have to (like doing a bad job for a paycheck).
- **Willing:** Being okay with or neutral about an experience, often doing it as a gift for someone else.
- **Wanting:** Actively desiring an experience for one's own pleasure and benefit.

Many developmental trauma survivors spend their entire lives enduring or tolerating, completely disconnected from their own wants. A core goal of therapy is helping clients build the capacity to experience what they actually want.

## 5. Somatic Practices for Re-Patterning

The session introduces several highly structured somatic games to help clients practice connection, pacing, and empowered choice:
- **Hello/Goodbye:** A gaze practice where participants make eye contact and practice saying "goodbye" (looking away or closing eyes) the moment they feel the impulse to break contact, and "hello" when they are ready to return. 
- **Mirroring:** One person leads with gestures, and the other follows. This fulfills the deep developmental need to be mimicked and to mimic, building attachment and synchronicity.
- **"When Your Body Does That, My Body Does This":** An improvised conversation of gestures and sounds that encourages spontaneity, play, and emergent relational dynamics.
