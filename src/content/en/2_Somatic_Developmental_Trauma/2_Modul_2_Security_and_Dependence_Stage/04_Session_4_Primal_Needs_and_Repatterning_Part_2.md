# Session 4: Primal Needs and Repatterning (Part 2)

In this session, Mariah Rooney and Kai Cheng Thom critically examine the concept of "regulation," introduce the dynamics of Shape and Counter-Shape, and explore how to hold therapeutic polarities safely.

## 1. Reconceptualizing "Regulation"

In contemporary psychology, "regulation" is often treated as a synonym for "calm down." However, forcing someone to calm down can be an oppressive form of control, particularly for marginalized populations who are frequently told their emotions are "too big."

- **The Danger of the Binary:** Viewing clients strictly as either "regulated" or "dysregulated" ignores the necessary spectrum of human emotion. What is often labeled as "dysregulation" is simply a person having a tough time or experiencing profound, necessary feelings like grief or rage.
- **Making Space for Bigness:** Healing developmental trauma is not about shrinking emotions to fit a socially acceptable window. It is about expanding the window of capacity so the client can safely express the trueness of how they feel. Righteous anger and deep wailing have a place in trauma recovery.

## 2. Shape and Counter-Shape

Development occurs through a dance of "Shape and Counter-Shape" between a child and their environment.
- **The Ideal Dance:** A baby expresses a shape (e.g., crying in distress, reaching out), and the caregiver provides the appropriate counter-shape (e.g., getting down on the floor, holding, soothing). The baby's job is simply to be their shape; the caregiver's job is to meet it.
- **The Traumatic Disruption:** Trauma occurs when the caregiver's counter-shape is harsh, rejecting, or absent. Over time, the child learns to alter *their* shape to appease the adult, sacrificing their authenticity to maintain the attachment.

In somatic therapy, the practitioner seeks to offer the missing counter-shape. This might look like accompaniment (sitting quietly nearby), celebration (cheering on a small victory), or offering protection and validation.

## 3. Holding Polarities

Developmental trauma often leaves clients trapped in profound internal conflicts or polarities (e.g., "I desperately want connection, but connection is terrifying").

- **Avoiding the Urge to Fix:** The goal is not to immediately resolve the polarity or force the client to choose a side.
- **The "Both/And" Approach:** Practitioners help clients build the capacity to hold multiple, seemingly opposing truths at the same time. By dropping into a "Wise Observer" state, the client's nervous system learns that it is safe to experience contradiction without fracturing.

## 4. Touch and Doing Our Own Work

When incorporating touch or deep somatic practices into therapy, practitioners must operate from a place of deep ethical accountability.
- **The Necessity of Personal Experience:** A practitioner cannot guide a client through deep somatic terrain if they have not traversed it themselves. Therapists must receive somatic touch work and therapy to truly understand the vulnerability and non-verbal dynamics involved.
- **Humility and Limits:** Practitioners must recognize when they do not have the energetic capacity to hold space for a client's specific trauma. Practicing ethical referrals and leaning on supervision networks is a core part of trauma-informed care.
