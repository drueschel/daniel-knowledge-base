# Session 2: Parts & Developmental Fragmentation

In this session, Mariah Rooney and Kai Cheng Thom explore the profound intelligence of dissociation and parts work as an adaptive response to trauma, neglect, and oppressive environments. They reframe fragmentation not as a pathology to be "cured," but as a brilliant survival strategy that requires deep respect.

## 1. The Spectrum of Dissociation

Dissociation is a normal, vital human capacity. It exists on a spectrum:
- **Mild Dissociation:** Daydreaming, "road amnesia" (driving home and not remembering the trip), or zoning out during a boring task.
- **Moderate Dissociation:** Numbing of certain physical sensations or emotions (e.g., feeling sad but feeling nothing in the body).
- **Severe Dissociation:** Dissociative amnesia, depersonalization (feeling disconnected from oneself), derealization (the world feels unreal), and structural fragmentation like Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID).

When an experience is too overwhelming or traumatic, the body and nervous system figure out how to "leave" the body to survive. This brilliant adaptation keeps people alive, but it can create significant challenges later in life when it causes chronic disconnection from the present moment.

## 2. The Energy-Action-Interaction-Meaning Cycle

Children learn how to make sense of their internal sensations through their interactions with caregivers:
1. **Energy/Sensation:** A baby feels a physical discomfort (e.g., a stomach ache).
2. **Action:** The baby cries to express the need.
3. **Interaction:** The caregiver responds attunedly (soothing, checking for a wet diaper) or misattunedly (yelling, ignoring).
4. **Meaning-Making:** Over time, the child internalizes what their bodily sensations mean and whether it is safe to express them.

If a child learns that expressing sadness leads to punishment, they don't stop feeling sad; instead, they unconsciously learn to *fragment* their experience. They develop a "part" of themselves that shuts down the sadness and presents an emotion that is acceptable to the family (like anger or compliance).

## 3. Sociopolitical Fragmentation

Fragmentation is not only a response to nuclear family dynamics. We also fragment ourselves to survive oppressive cultures. 
- A queer child in a highly conservative environment might develop a "heterosexual-presenting" part to stay safe and belong. 
- A Black or Brown person navigating a racist society might develop parts to mask their authentic expressions of grief or rage to avoid violence.
- Capitalism rewards workaholism and perfectionism, encouraging us to develop parts that relentlessly drive productivity while exiling parts that need rest.

## 4. The Goal of Parts Work: Cohesion, Not Integration

In therapeutic work involving parts (whether through Internal Family Systems, Psychomotor Therapy, or other modalities), the language used to describe parts should always be tailored to the client (e.g., parts, alters, selves, headmates). 

Crucially, the goal of parts work is **NOT integration** (forcing all parts to merge into a single, uniform identity). Telling a plural system that success means becoming "one person" is experienced as an erasure of their internal community. 

Instead, the goal is **Cohesion and Collaboration**. It is about fostering communication, curiosity, and teamwork among the different parts so that the internal system can work together in the present day, ensuring no part is left behind or exiled.

## 5. The Therapist's Stance

To do this work effectively, practitioners must:
- Honor the historical wisdom of every part. Every part, no matter how destructive its current behavior (e.g., substance use, self-harm, people-pleasing), developed to protect the system.
- Refrain from forcing an agenda. Parts work must move at the speed of the most hesitant part.
- Do their own internal parts work to ensure their counter-transference (like a "Rescuer" part or an "Academic/Scientist" part) does not override the client's agency.
