# Session 1: The Pre-Perinatal Continuum: An Overview

This session, led by Dr. Arielle Schwartz, Manuela Mischke-Reeds, and Dr. Scott Lyons, provides a foundational overview of developmental trauma, specifically focusing on the pre- and perinatal periods (from in-utero development to age three). It introduces core definitions, the "Trauma Tree" model, and the somatic and emotional indicators of early trauma.

## 1. Defining Trauma and Developmental Trauma

- **General Definition of Trauma:** Drawing on Peter Levine’s work, trauma is defined as an experience that is "too much too soon," "too much for too long," or, in the case of neglect, "not enough for too long" or "too little for too long." It is an experience that overwhelms the ordinary human capacity to adapt (Judith Herman), resulting in a profound loss of agency, power, and a sense of helplessness.
- **Trauma as a Thwarted Instinct:** Trauma occurs when primal protective instincts (reaching for connection, calling for help, fleeing, or setting a boundary) are thwarted and unable to complete. The body and psyche remain stuck seeking a sense of resolution.
- **The Trauma Tree:** A visual metaphor for understanding how trauma manifests:
  - **Leaves (Top):** The presenting symptoms and challenges (overwhelm, numbness, immune dysregulation, chronic pain, addictions).
  - **Trunk (Core):** Adaptive responses and compensations. These are the habits, behaviors, and physical shapes formed around the wounding to survive and hold the fragments of self together.
  - **Roots (Beneath):** The origination of the wounding (shock, relational disruptions, cultural/systemic violations, and developmental trauma).

## 2. The Nature of Developmental Trauma

Developmental trauma is the interruption or fragmentation of the natural, sequential psychobiological stages of human growth. 

- **Sequential and Relational:** Development unfolds through a natural sequence of rhythms (activation and rest, dependency and independence, horizontal to vertical movement). Crucially, development is a deeply relational process. A baby develops in relation to their primary caregivers, their environment, gravity, and space.
- **The Impact of Chronicity:** When young children (ages 0 to 3) face chronic, repetitive exposure to stress, misattunement, or harmful experiences, the natural rhythm of being tended to is disrupted. This disrupts the foundational blueprint for how they relate to themselves, others, and the world.
- **The Pre-Cognitive Brain:** In these early stages, the brain is formed but neural connections are still rapidly developing based on sensory input. Trauma during this pre-verbal phase directly disrupts sensory perception and the body's baseline of safety, leading to deep somatic imprints rather than cognitive memories.

## 3. Indicators of Developmental Trauma

Because trauma from the pre- and perinatal phases is pre-verbal, it does not present as conscious narrative memories. Instead, it presents as "indicators" or somatic signatures left in the body.

### Somatic Indicators
- **Primal Freeze:** Stilled, held, or shallow breathing. The breath capacity is perceived as very restricted.
- **Heightened Sensitivity:** Extreme sensitivity to sound, touch, or pain. The nervous system may react with high alarm even to consensual touch.
- **Spatial Disorientation:** Feeling claustrophobic, anxious, or panicky in confined spaces (often linked to early birth trauma). A sense of fogginess or inability to reference one's own internal body experience.

### Emotional and Relational Indicators
- **Existential Themes:** Feelings of annihilation, not deserving to exist, or not being wanted. A pervasive, global feeling that "life itself is unsafe."
- **Time and Transition Confusion:** Chronic lateness, inability to read the clock, and extreme difficulty with transitions (e.g., lingering at the end of therapy sessions). This stems from disrupted primal rhythms.
- **Miscuing Social Cues:** Misinterpreting warm or neutral social interactions as threats (e.g., perceiving a smile as an aggressive showing of teeth).
- **Relational Ambivalence:** A profound yearning for connection paired simultaneously with a dismissal or fear of closeness ("I want it, but I can't take it"). 

## 4. The Practice of Anchoring

Healing from pre- and perinatal trauma requires navigating "watery" and disorienting internal landscapes. To prevent the nervous system from becoming lost or re-traumatized, clients must first establish safety through **Anchoring**.

- **External Anchors:** Using the five senses to orient to the present environment (e.g., looking at an item of beauty, holding a comforting object like a sandbag, tasting tea).
- **Internal Anchors:** Utilizing interoceptive awareness, such as sensing the breath, noticing posture, or finding a specific location in the body that feels like "home."
- **Proprioceptive Anchors:** Sensing the body's weight in relationship to gravity. This can involve making a specific physical gesture, holding the face/heart/belly, or engaging in small movements like swaying or stretching.
- **Mental Anchors:** Using an intention, a safe word, or a phrase (e.g., "courage," "connection," "belonging") to remind the cognitive mind why it is safe to engage in the healing process.
